HackFax x PatriotHacks 2026: post-mortem

2026-02-17 · 7 min read · ctf · leadership · web-security

Three years ago I showed up to HackFax as a participant. I solved 50 challenges, got humbled by an OSINT track that needed me to read a Russian PDF, and went home thinking "next year I want to write one of these." This year I led the team that wrote 30 of them. Here is what I learned.

The event

HackFax x PatriotHacks 2026 - Valentine's Day Hackathon + CTF. 36 hours, February 13 to 15, across Dewberry, Horizon, the Johnson Center, and the MIX Media Lab. The hackathon side and the CTF side ran in parallel. You could compete in both. Free, open to all skill levels, fully sponsored.

The theme was Valentine's Day, and we leaned into it. The CTF was pink, full of hearts and Cupid and Valentine puns. Challenge names like Unsent Valentine, Cupid's Heartgram, Love in the Source, Locked Letter, The Architect's Love, Cupid Cipher. I wanted the aesthetic to pull beginners in instead of scaring them off.

The arc

2024: participant. Solved 50 across OSINT, Cyber Principles, Web Attacks.
2025: challenge developer in the Web Attack category.
2026: Web Attack lead. Team of around 10 designers. Six months of build. 30 challenges shipped. Mason's biggest CTF to date.

What worked

  • Difficulty pyramid per author. Each designer shipped an Easy, a Medium, and a Hard. That gave the track a balanced shape and made sure no one author owned the whole "this challenge is impossible" cluster.
  • Pair-testing before launch. Every challenge got a "second attacker" who tried to solve it cold without the author in the room. Caught roughly a third of our broken intent ratios. The author always thinks their challenge is fine.
  • A shared "dirty tricks" doc. One Notion page with every cheap trick we agreed not to use. Zero-width characters in flags, off-by-one in regex, sub-pixel click targets, hiding flags in the Vercel build log. Saved us a lot of tickets.
  • Vercel for everything web. Every challenge was its own static Vercel deployment with a clean URL like hidden-in-plain-sight.vercel.app or cupid-cipher.vercel.app. Zero infra to babysit. Push to ship. Players got fast pages. We got rollbacks for free.

What I would change next time

  • More testers, earlier. Half our late-stage bugs came from "the only person who tried this challenge was the author." A test rotation in week four would have caught the rest.
  • Fewer cute tricks. A challenge that takes 90 minutes because the intended path is brilliant feels great when you solve it and miserable when you do not. The good ones reward thought, not pattern-matching to the author's sense of humor.
  • Hint economy from day one. We launched without a hint system, then bolted one on Saturday afternoon when the leaderboard stalled. Should have shipped cost-per-hint balanced against challenge points from the start.
  • Beginner ramp at the very top of the queue. The first three challenges a player tries decide whether they stay. Easy challenges should be SO easy that anyone who can use a browser solves one in five minutes. We had this for some authors, not all. Hidden in Plain Sight was the model: open DevTools, read the page title, done. That is the right shape for an entry point.

What it taught me about security work

The thing I did not expect: leading a CTF made me a better defender. When you have spent six months thinking about how to hide a flag in a deliberately broken auth flow, you start seeing the same shapes in real production code. Most of the bugs I have caught at work since then are things that would have been a 200-point challenge.

The best CTF challenge is one that teaches you to recognize a class of bug, not just to solve a puzzle.
