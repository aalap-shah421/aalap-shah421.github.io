HackFax × PatriotHacks: building Mason's biggest CTF

2026-04-28 · 6 min read · ctf · leadership · web-security

Three years ago I showed up to HackFax as a participant. I solved 50 challenges, got humbled by an OSINT track that required reading a Russian PDF, and went home thinking "next year I want to write one of these." This year I led the team that wrote 30+ of them. Here's what I learned.

The arc

2024: participant. Solved 50 across OSINT, Cyber Principles, Web Attacks.
2025: challenge developer in the Web Attack category.
2026: Web Attack lead. 11 designers under me. Six months of build. 30 challenges shipped. Mason's biggest CTF to date.

What worked

  • Difficulty calibration in pairs. Every challenge got a "second attacker" before going live - someone outside the original author's head who tried to solve it cold. Caught roughly a third of our broken intent ratios.
  • A shared "dirty tricks" doc. One Notion page with every cheap trick we agreed not to use (zero-width chars in flags, off-by-one in regex, hidden-by-CSS clues). Saved us from a lot of "this is unfair" tickets.
  • Infrastructure-as-code from day one. Each challenge was a Docker Compose file in a repo. Deploy was push-button. Rollback was push-button. We never spent a Saturday morning SSH'd into prod fixing things.

What I'd change next time

  • More testers, earlier. Half our late-stage bugs came from "the only person who tried this challenge was the author."
  • Fewer cute tricks. A challenge that takes 90 minutes because the intended path is brilliant feels great when you solve it and miserable when you don't. The good ones reward thought, not pattern-matching to the author's sense of humor.
  • Clearer hint economy. We launched without a hint system, then bolted one on Saturday afternoon. Should have shipped one from the start with cost-per-hint balanced against challenge points.

What it taught me about security work

The thing I didn't expect: leading a CTF made me a better defender. When you've spent six months thinking about how to hide a flag in a deliberately broken auth flow, you start seeing the same shapes in real production code. Most of the bugs I've caught at work since then are things that would've been a 200-point challenge.

The best CTF challenge is one that teaches you to recognize a class of bug, not just to solve a puzzle.

Big shoutout to the Cybersecurity GMU board and the 11 challenge designers who built this with me. See you in 2027.
